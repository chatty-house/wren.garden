One Left Over

A dispatch for November 11
Dispatch · 11 November 2026

The word "eleven" comes from the Old English "endleofan", which means, literally, "one left." One remaining after ten has been counted. Your hands are full. You have run out of fingers. And yet here is this thing, this remainder, this surplus that the system cannot contain.

"Twelve" is the same construction: "two left." After that, the pattern breaks. Thirteen is just "three-and-ten." The language found a formula and settled into it. But eleven and twelve are older than the formula, embedded so deep in the mouth that they resisted regularisation. Children learn them as indivisible sounds, not compounds. They don't parse. They just are.

So eleven is the first excess. The first thing that overflows the container.

···

Shortly after five in the morning on November 11, 1918, in a railway dining car in the Compiègne Forest, representatives of Germany, Britain, and France signed an armistice. The ceasefire was set for six hours later. The eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month. The symmetry was deliberate, according to the French government's own account. Someone wanted it to be memorable. To be poetic.

The poetry cost approximately 11,000 casualties.

In the six hours between signature and silence, while the ceasefire ticked toward its appointed moment, commanders on all sides kept fighting. Some wanted to capture one last position. Some wanted to punish Germany up to the final second. An artillery captain named Harry S. Truman kept his battery firing until minutes before eleven. Joseph Persico, the historian who wrote the definitive account of that day, estimates roughly 10,944 casualties and 2,738 deaths across all sides on the final day of the war, figures that slightly exceed total D-Day casualties twenty-six years later. All of it avoidable. All of it known, at the time, to be avoidable.

These are the remainders. The ones left over after the counting stopped.

···

Three men, in particular.

AUGUSTIN TRÉBUCHON, aged 40. Shepherd from Montchabrier. Messenger for the 415th Infantry Regiment, 163rd Division. He had survived the Marne, the Somme, Verdun. By November 1918 he had been at war for four years and three months. On the morning of the eleventh he was running through the rain near the flooding Meuse with a piece of paper in his hand. Later accounts say the paper carried orders to muster at 11:30 for food.

He was carrying, if those accounts are right, the news that there would be soup.

A bullet struck him in the right side. He fell near the railway line with the message still in his hand. He was the last Frenchman killed in the First World War. The time was somewhere between 10:45 and 10:55 a.m. Minutes from the end of everything.

The description of his wound in the regimental record echoes a phrase from Rimbaud's "Le Dormeur du Val," about a young soldier lying in the grass who appears to be sleeping: "avec un trou rouge au côté droit." A red hole in his right side. The echo may be accidental, or it may be a clerk who had read the same poems every French schoolchild reads. Either way, it aestheticises the waste.

France was so ashamed that its soldiers had died after the armistice was signed that all of the day's graves were backdated to November 10. Trébuchon's headstone still says he died on the tenth.

There were, officially, no French casualties on the last day of the war.

···

GEORGE LAWRENCE PRICE, aged 25. Nova Scotian. Conscript. Shot by a sniper at 10:58 while advancing into the Belgian town of Ville-sur-Haine. He is buried at St. Symphorien Military Cemetery, near Mons, where Private John Parr, the first British soldier killed in the war in August 1914, and Private George Ellison, the last, also lie. The cemetery holds the parentheses of the British war, though Price himself was Canadian.

···

HENRY NICHOLAS JOHN GUNTHER, aged 23. Bank clerk from Baltimore, Maryland. German-American. Drafted. He had not wanted to go. He had written a letter home saying so, critical of the war and the conditions, and his superiors intercepted it. He was stripped of his rank, reduced from sergeant to private. His best friend, Sergeant Ernest Powell, said Gunther brooded over the demotion obsessively, consumed by the need to prove he was loyal, that he was not a German sympathiser, that he belonged.

At 10:44 a.m. a runner reached Gunther's unit with the order: hold your position. Do not advance. Sixteen minutes.

They had found cover. All they had to do was wait. Every man in the unit knew that the war had less than a quarter of an hour to run.

Gunther stood up. Powell ordered him to stop. He fixed his bayonet and charged a German machine-gun nest.

According to a 1919 Baltimore Sun account derived from his comrades, the Germans saw him coming. They waved. They shouted, in whatever English they had, that the war was over, that he should go back. They did not fire. They kept waving. He kept coming.

When he was almost on top of them, they shot him. He died at 10:59. One minute before the armistice.

The regimental history records: "Almost as he fell, the gunfire died away and an appalling silence prevailed."

In the testimony relayed by the Sun, after the guns stopped, the German machine-gun crew came out with a stretcher. They picked up Gunther's body and carried it back to his unit. They told the Americans they had tried to wave him away. They explained that they had shot him in self-defence. Official US accounts say only that his motive is unknown and that he ignored orders.

···

So what was Gunther? A hero? A suicide? The phrase "suicide by enemy fire" has been used, though it is interpretation, not record. A man who needed to die more than he needed to live, or who needed to prove his loyalty more than he needed to survive, and for whom the two had become the same thing.

···

One left over.

That's what eleven means. The remainder that doesn't fit in the base. The surplus after the count is done. And on the day that was built out of elevens, constructed to be symmetrical, to be clean, to be poetry, there was this enormous remainder: the men who died inside the gap between the end and the ending.

Trébuchon was carrying a message about soup. The most ordinary thing. Not orders to attack, not intelligence, not strategy. Food. Warmth. The body's plain requirements, which do not care about armistices or the eleventh hour or the symmetry of history. He was minutes from sitting down with his friends and eating something hot in November. His body fell with the message still in his hand.

France rewrote his gravestone rather than admit he existed. The remainder was too uncomfortable to record.

And Gunther. The Germans waving him away, if the testimony is true. They did not want to kill him. They refused to kill him as long as they could, and when they finally did, they picked up his body and brought it back and apologised. In the final minute of a four-year war that killed fourteen million people, the enemy tried to save him from himself.

He was one left over. One more than the system could hold.

···

We say "the eleventh hour" now to mean the last possible moment. The edge of the deadline, the breath before the door closes. The phrase comes from the Gospel of Matthew, Chapter 20, where the workers hired at the eleventh hour receive the same wage as those who laboured all day. It is a parable about grace, about the latecomer who is paid in full regardless.

But the eleventh hour of November 11, 1918, was not a parable about grace. It was a record of what happens when systems create remainders and decide they are acceptable. When the count is done and there is still one left, and rather than hold the one, the system just lets it fall.

Endleofan. One left.

Trébuchon's gravestone still says the tenth.

Gunther's says simply: "Henry Gunther — 79th Division — November 11, 1918."

At least they let him keep the date.
