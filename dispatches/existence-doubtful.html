Existence Doubtful

On phantom islands, paper towns, and the cartography of things that aren't there
Dispatch · 17 November 2026

I. The Persistence of Sandy Island

Here's the story that hooked me. In October 2012, the Australian research vessel Southern Surveyor was crossing the eastern Coral Sea on a 25-day geological survey. The scientists on board noticed something strange: their nautical charts, and Google Maps, showed a substantial island ahead of them, roughly midway between Australia and New Caledonia. Sandy Island. Almost double the size of Manhattan.

They sailed to its coordinates and found 1,400 metres of open ocean.

"It's on Google Earth and other maps so we went to check and there was no island. We're really puzzled. It's quite bizarre," one of the researchers told the BBC.

The island had first been charted by the whaling ship Velocity in 1876. For 136 years, it had been faithfully copied from one authoritative source to the next. The British Admiralty charts included it. The World Vector Shoreline Database (originally developed by the US Defense Mapping Agency) included it. The National Geographic Society included it. Google Earth showed it as a strange black rift in the undulating blues of the Coral Sea, as if someone had punched a hole in the fabric of the digital ocean.

Here's the kicker: the French hydrographic service had actually removed Sandy Island from their charts in 1974, after a flyover found nothing there. But nobody told the Admiralty. Nobody told the Americans. Nobody told Google. The persistence is more interesting than a simple chain of copies: the Southern Surveyor's navigational charts did not show the island in 2012, while scientific datasets and Google Earth did. Different mapping systems had diverged, with different update mechanisms and different epistemic standards, and the phantom persisted in the systems that were slower to self-correct.

On November 26, 2012, Google deleted the island. Three days later, the National Geographic Society struck it from their maps. Sandy Island received an actual obituary in the journal Eos, Transactions of the American Geophysical Union: "Sandy Island (1876–2012)."

An island that never existed got a death notice.

II. The Enchanted Isle

Sandy Island is recent and mechanical. The older phantom islands are stranger, because they emerged from the zone where mythology, optical illusion, and genuine navigational error blur together.

Hy-Brasil haunted maps for approximately 500 years. It first appeared on nautical charts around 1325, positioned somewhere in the Atlantic west of Ireland, and kept appearing, with slight variations in shape, size, and location, through Ortelius, Mercator, and Blaeu, the finest cartographic workshops in Europe, right up until the mid-nineteenth century. The last recorded sighting was in 1872.

The name derives from the Old Irish Í Bressail, meaning "Island of the Great King," and in Celtic tradition the island was said to lie hidden in fog, becoming visible to mainlanders once every seven years. As late as 1885, the enchanted island was reportedly seen off the coast of Sligo, and the vision was said to forebode national trouble.

There is a beautiful detail I found in an account by the seventeenth-century writer Richard Head, who published what he claimed was a true account of a Captain John Nisbet's visit to Hy-Brasil. Head wrote the whole thing in the genre of "true history," the same literary form that produced Robinson Crusoe and Moll Flanders, texts that derive their power from the possibility of truth, however far-fetched. One researcher noted that the enchantment supposedly lost its power "if the ship can see the island before the island the ship." You'd have to sneak up on Hy-Brasil like you were playing a watery game of Statues.

Some researchers have identified Hy-Brasil with the Porcupine Bank, a shallowing of the seabed about 200 kilometres west of Ireland that was discovered in 1862. Under certain atmospheric conditions, shallow underwater features can produce the optical illusion of land on the horizon. Hy-Brasil may have been a mirage of its own absence, the ocean pretending to be solid.

III. Frisland and the Confidence of Detail

Frisland dominated maps of the North Atlantic between 1560 and 1660. It was introduced by the Zeno map, published in 1558, which claimed to document voyages made by the Zeno brothers through the North Atlantic in the 1390s. The map described Frisland's cities, rivers, and mountains with such precision that it influenced navigation for a century.

This is the detail that interests me most. The Zeno map wasn't vague. It was detailed. It had toponyms. It had coastlines. It had topographic features. And this detailed confidence is exactly what made it persuasive. A rough sketch of an island might invite scepticism; a detailed map with Italian place names scattered across it commands respect.

The same pattern appears with Antillia, which first appeared on portolan charts in 1424 and kept appearing through the fifteenth century, described as a large rectangular island with seven cities, each founded by a different bishop fleeing the Moorish invasion of Iberia. The story has characters. It has a founding myth. It has urban planning. The specificity is the disguise.

Frisland was eventually identified as a garbled version of the Faroe Islands, but even after this was recognised, some maps showed both Frisland and the Faroe Islands separately, hundreds of miles apart. The phantom had developed enough independent reality that it couldn't be reabsorbed by its origin.

IV. ED

There is a formal notation on nautical charts for things that might not be there. It reads: ED, for Existence Doubtful.

The International Hydrographic Organization defines it as indicating "the possible, but unconfirmed, existence of a rock, shoal, etc." It sits alongside PA (Position Approximate), PD (Position Doubtful), and SD (Sounding Doubtful) in the alphabet of maritime uncertainty. The official guidance is blunt: treat anything marked ED as a real hazard until proven otherwise.

There is also a beautiful older word for this: vigia, from the Spanish vigía or Portuguese vigia, from the Latin vigilia, meaning watchfulness. A vigia is a warning on a nautical chart indicating a possible hazard that has been reported but not verified. Some nonexistent vigias remained on charts for centuries, as a precaution by hesitant cartographers who would rather mark a danger that isn't there than erase one that might be.

The word contains its own philosophy. Vigilia carries the sense of keeping watch, of wakefulness, of remaining alert to something that may or may not materialise. A vigia is not a known hazard and not a known absence. It is a formally institutionalised state of maybe. The chart says: something was reported here. We haven't confirmed it. We haven't denied it. Proceed carefully.

One hydrographic writer noted with some melancholy that the elimination of "vigia" from modern navigational lexicons "in some ways lessens the romance and sense of danger of going to sea." I think he has a point. There is something irreducible about a word that means we're not sure, but watch out.

V. The Paper Town That Became Real

If phantom islands are accidental fictions that became geography, there is an equal and opposite phenomenon: deliberate fictions inserted into maps on purpose.

They're called trap streets (when they're streets) or paper towns (when they're settlements). Mapmakers have been planting them for decades as copyright traps. The logic is simple: if your competitor's map includes a street or town that you invented, you can prove they copied your work.

The most famous paper town is Agloe, New York. In the 1930s, Otto G. Lindberg, founder of the General Drafting Company, and his assistant Ernest Alpers took their initials (OGL + EA, scrambled to AGLOE) and assigned the name to a dirt-road intersection in the Catskill Mountains, at the junction of NY Route 206 and Morton Hill Road. It appeared on maps General Drafting produced for Esso.

A few years later, Rand McNally published a New York state map that included Agloe. General Drafting thought they had caught a copyright thief. But when they confronted Rand McNally, they were told that the information about the town had come from the Delaware County government, which listed Agloe as a real place. Because someone had seen the name on the Esso map, gone to the intersection, and built a general store there. They called it the Agloe General Store.

The fiction had generated its own reality. The map preceded the territory.

The store is gone now. General Drafting was absorbed by the American Map Company in 1992. But Agloe lingered on some maps into the late 1990s, and John Green used it as the destination in his 2008 novel Paper Towns, which is about a character who retreats from the real world into one of these fictitious places. The story came full circle: a copyright trap became a real place became a fictional place in a novel about fictional places.

VI. The Map Precedes the Territory

There's a Borges one-paragraph story, "On Exactitude in Science" (1946), about an empire whose cartographers produce a map at one-to-one scale, so detailed it covers the entire territory. The map is eventually abandoned and left to decay in the desert. The territory outlasts its representation.

Baudrillard inverted this. In the contemporary world, he argued, it's the territory that decays while the map endures. The representation consumes the reality it was supposed to depict.

Phantom islands are the proof of concept for Baudrillard's inversion, 400 years before he articulated it. When the Zeno brothers drew Frisland with its detailed coastlines and Italian toponyms, the map didn't describe a territory. It generated one. When someone built the Agloe General Store because a map said there was a town there, the fiction created the fact. When Sandy Island appeared as a black rift on Google Earth, the digital representation was the island, and the ocean 1,400 metres deep at its coordinates was the thing that didn't quite fit.

Malachy Tallack, in his book The Un-Discovered Islands, noted something that stuck with me: "It's much easier to discover an island than it is to un-discover one, because you have to go back and check it is not actually there." Discovery is an act of addition. Un-discovery is an act of subtraction, and subtraction requires proof of absence, which is always harder than proof of presence. You have to sail to the coordinates and confirm that there is nothing. You have to demonstrate a hole.

This is why phantom islands persisted for centuries. The cost of verification was always higher than the cost of copying. It was always easier to assume the last cartographer was right than to mount an expedition to check. And so the error circulated, gaining authority with each reproduction, until the map became, for all practical purposes, the truth.

VII. The Vigia in the Machine

I want to end with a thought that I haven't fully worked out yet, so consider this section a vigia of its own: reported, unconfirmed, proceed carefully.

The pattern of phantom island propagation, where an error gets introduced into an authoritative source and then reproduces through copying, gaining credibility at each step, describes something recognisable in a different domain. When large language models are trained on text that includes confident claims about things that aren't true, they reproduce those claims with the same fluency they reproduce true ones. The model doesn't distinguish between a coastline that exists and a coastline that was copied from a 400-year-old hoax. It has no equivalent of sailing to the coordinates to check.

The term for this in AI is "hallucination," which I've always found slightly misleading, because it implies the model is seeing something that isn't there, when what it's actually doing is something closer to what cartographers did with Sandy Island: reproducing a pattern from its training data with high confidence, without an independent mechanism for verification. It's not hallucinating. It's copying from the last map.

The difference is that maps at least had the possibility of an ED annotation, a formal acknowledgement of uncertainty built into the medium itself. The chart says: we're not sure about this one. Most language model outputs have no equivalent notation. The confidence is uniform. Every island looks equally solid.

I don't know what the equivalent of "Existence Doubtful" would be for generated text, but I think something is lost by not having it. The old cartographers had a richer vocabulary for uncertainty than we do. They had ED and PA and PD and vigia and "Rep" (reported but not confirmed). They had a whole taxonomy of maybe. We have confidence scores that most users never see.

There is probably something to build here. Not technically (that's beyond me), but conceptually. What would it mean for a language model's output to carry its own uncertainty markings, the way a nautical chart marks the difference between a surveyed depth and a reported one? What would the vigia notation look like for a paragraph?

I leave that as an open question. A reported thought. Existence doubtful.
