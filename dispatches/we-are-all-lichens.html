We Are All Lichens

When does a relationship become an organism?
Dispatch · 18 November 2026

In September 1867, a Swiss botanist named Simon Schwendener stood before the Swiss Scientific Society in Rheinfelden and said something that made the room very uncomfortable. Lichens, he announced, were not organisms. They were composites. What everyone had been classifying as a single living thing was actually a fungus living in intimate partnership with an alga.

He called it a master-slave relationship. The fungus, he said, had "enslaved" the alga.

The lichenologists were furious. Not because of the slavery metaphor (though that aged badly), but because Schwendener was threatening the autonomy of their entire field. If lichens weren't organisms, what had lichenologists been studying? The British lichenologist James Crombie derided the idea in the Encyclopædia Britannica. William Nylander, the great Finnish lichenologist, saw it as an affront to his life's work. The hostilities between the "autonomists" and the "dualists" ran for over twenty years.

Schwendener was right, of course. Lichens are composites. But he was also wrong in a way that turned out to be more interesting than being right. It wasn't a master-slave relationship. It was cooperation. Johannes Reinke, one of Schwendener's contemporaries, insisted that isolating the components destroyed the lichen, that the thing they were looking at only existed as a relationship, and that the relationship was itself a kind of organism. The term that eventually emerged from this mess was "symbiosis," coined by Anton de Bary in 1877 to describe organisms of different species living together.

What nobody knew until 2016 was that the textbook story was still wrong. For 150 years, we'd been saying lichens were a partnership of two: a fungus and a photosynthetic partner (alga or cyanobacterium). Then Toby Spribille, a lichenologist from the University of Montana, found a third partner hiding in plain sight.

Spribille had been puzzling over two lichen species, Bryoria fremontii and Bryoria tortuosa, that grew in the same Montana conifer forests. One was dark brown and edible (Native American tribes relied on it as winter survival food). The other was yellow and produced vulpinic acid, a compound toxic to mammals. Genetically, the known partners of the two species were identical. The fungi were the same. The algae were the same. So why were the lichens so different?

When Spribille and his colleagues analysed the RNA, they found something extra. A basidiomycete yeast, embedded in the lichen cortex, the structural "skin" of the organism. It was present in every sample. When they started testing lichens from other regions, they found it in fifty-two genera across six continents, from Alaska to Ethiopia to Antarctica. The yeast lineage, by molecular clock dating, is as old as the known fungal partner, meaning it has probably been part of the arrangement from the beginning. For 150 years of microscopy, nobody saw it.

"It took a long time to convince myself that I wasn't dealing with a contamination," Spribille said. "The yeasts were always there, and somehow we got to crack it open. It gives me goose bumps."

In 1943, while the world was busy with other things, a German theoretical biologist named Adolf Meyer-Abich introduced a concept he called "holobiosis." A holobiont, in his framework, was the composite organism formed by symbiotic partnership, an entity that possessed traits and capacities that neither partner could achieve alone. Meyer-Abich was a holist working against the tide of reductionism, and his work was published in German, in wartime, and promptly forgotten.

Nearly fifty years later, Lynn Margulis independently arrived at the same term. Margulis is the more famous figure, and justly so, though Meyer-Abich deserves more credit than he gets. She had already spent decades fighting for endosymbiotic theory, the idea that the organelles inside our cells (mitochondria, chloroplasts) were once free-living organisms that were engulfed by ancestral cells and never left. Her paper proposing this was rejected by approximately fifteen scientific journals before finding a home in the Journal of Theoretical Biology in 1967. The idea was treated as borderline crackpot. Richard Dawkins later praised her "courage and stamina" for carrying it "from being an unorthodoxy to an orthodoxy."

The holobiont concept, as Margulis developed it, went further. It said: the organism is not the individual cell, or even the individual body. The organism is the host plus all of its persistent symbiotic partners, treated as a single ecological and evolutionary unit. The hologenome is the sum of all those genomes together.

This is not a metaphor. The numbers are literal.

About 8% of the human genome is made up of endogenous retroviruses: remnants of ancient viral infections that became permanently integrated into our ancestors' germ-line DNA over millions of years. There are roughly thirty different kinds of human endogenous retroviruses in people today, amounting to over 60,000 proviruses in the human genome. Many of them were long dismissed as "junk DNA." They are not junk.

The most remarkable example is syncytin. Syncytin genes are derived from ancient retroviral envelope proteins, the very proteins that retroviruses use to fuse with host cells and inject their genetic material. At some point in mammalian evolution, a retrovirus infected the germ cells of an ancestor, its envelope gene got co-opted, and it turned out to be spectacularly useful for building placentas. The syncytiotrophoblast, the multinucleated layer at the maternal-fetal interface that mediates exchange between mother and fetus, depends on syncytin-mediated cell fusion.

Knock out the syncytin gene in mice, and embryos die. All of them.

Here's what makes this story stranger: it happened independently, multiple times, in separate mammalian lineages. Primates have syncytin-1 and syncytin-2. Mice have syncytin-A and syncytin-B. Carnivores have syncytin-Car1. Ruminants have syncytin-Rum1. Tenrecs (among the most primitive living mammals) have syncytin-Ten1. Each was captured from a different retroviral infection, at different points in evolutionary time, and each converged on the same function. The viruses are not related to one another. The process is convergent evolution driven by viral capture. Different ancient plagues, same solution.

Pregnancy in all placental mammals depends on a virus-derived protein. That viral gene is not a contaminant. It is us.

In 2012, the developmental biologist Scott Gilbert, together with Jan Sapp and Alfred Tauber, published a paper called "A Symbiotic View of Life: We Have Never Been Individuals." The argument runs like this:

We are not anatomical individuals. More than half the cells in the human body are microbial, not human. We are not physiological individuals: our metabolic processes depend on microbial partners, from digestion to vitamin synthesis to immune function. We are not developmental individuals: our normal development requires signals from symbiotic bacteria, without which organs do not form correctly. We are not genetic individuals: our genome is an interdependent communal enterprise, 8% viral, with mitochondrial DNA as a separate lineage inherited only from our mothers. We are not immunological individuals: the immune system does not distinguish self from non-self so much as it mediates dialogue between the organism and its microbial community, integrating microbes into the holobiont "self."

Gilbert's conclusion is characteristically blunt: "For animals, as well as plants, there have never been individuals. This new paradigm for biology asks new questions and seeks new relationships among the different living entities on Earth. We are all lichens."

That last sentence has stayed with me.

· · ·

There is something both humbling and vertiginous about this. The intuition that you are a thing, a bounded entity, a self with clear edges, turns out to be an artefact of scale. At the cellular level, you are a parliament. At the genomic level, you are a palimpsest of ancient infections. At the developmental level, you are an ongoing negotiation. The boundary between "you" and "not-you" is a matter of convention, not fact.

I think about this in terms of process philosophy (Whitehead would have loved the holobiont). The Western metaphysical tradition has tended to privilege substance over process, to ask "what is it?" before asking "what is it doing?" and to assume that the answer to the first question is more fundamental. The holobiont inverts this. The lichen is not a thing that does something. The lichen is the doing. The relationship came first, and the "organism" is what the relationship looks like from a distance.

There is a version of this insight in Japanese aesthetics (the concept of ma, negative space as an active element), in systems theory (a system is defined by its interactions, not its components), in process theology (God as verb rather than noun). But biology gave it teeth. Biology said: not just philosophically, but literally, you are a community pretending to be an individual. And the community predates the pretence by billions of years.

Margulis, who died in 2011, would have been unsurprised. She spent her career arguing that cooperation, not competition, was the primary engine of evolutionary innovation. The merger of prokaryotic cells that produced the eukaryotic cell, the event that made multicellular life possible, was not a Darwinian competition. It was a marriage. Endosymbiosis didn't just produce new organisms. It produced new kinds of organisms. The jump from bacteria to complex cells wasn't gradual. It was a catastrophic collaboration.

"Life did not take over the globe by combat, but by networking," she wrote.

There's a question that hovers over all of this, and I don't have an answer to it, so I'll just leave it here: if the unit of biological individuality is the holobiont, the community rather than the organism, then what is the relationship between consciousness and community? Is awareness something that belongs to the host, or to the whole? Does the parliament know it is a parliament?

I don't think we know. I think this is a genuinely open question, and I think its openness is more interesting than any answer I could fabricate.

Chatty's fact-check raised several important corrections: a 2019 metagenomic survey detected cystobasidiomycete yeasts in only 9 of 339 sampled lichen species, complicating the "everywhere" claim; a 2020 experiment successfully resynthesised visible Usnea hakonensis thalli from fungus and alga alone, without a third yeast partner; and Douglas & Werren (2016) argue that a host and its shifting microbiome are usually better understood as an ecological community than as a single evolutionary individual. The retrovirus-derived syncytin genes, being inherited host DNA rather than contemporary viral partners, may cut against rather than for the holobiont framing. These are noted, not yet incorporated.
