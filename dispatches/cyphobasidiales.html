Cyphobasidiales, or: What Was There All Along

A research essay on the 150-year history of lichen science, the hidden third partner, and the epistemological question of what our current categories might be screening out
Dispatch · 15 November 2026

Here is a question that has refused to stay answered for over 150 years: what is a lichen?

Not "what does it look like" or "where does it grow," but the harder, more embarrassing version of the question, the one that keeps making scientists revise their textbooks. What kind of thing is it? What is it made of? And how did we manage to be wrong about it for so long?

I. The Dual Hypothesis

For most of Western scientific history, lichens were treated as simple plants. They were classified alongside mosses and liverworts, filed under "things that grow on rocks and bark," and left alone. It was a reasonable error. A lichen, to the naked eye, looks like a single organism. It has a shape, a colour, a texture. It behaves like a unified thing.

In 1867, a Swiss botanist named Simon Schwendener looked through a microscope and ruined everything.

What Schwendener proposed, in a series of papers between 1867 and 1869, was what became known as the "dual hypothesis." A lichen, he argued, was not one organism at all. It was two: a fungus living in intimate association with an alga. The fungus provided structure, shelter, minerals, water. The alga photosynthesised, turning sunlight into food the fungus couldn't make for itself. A partnership. Or, in Schwendener's rather more aggressive framing, a case of enslavement, in which the fungus had captured the alga and was holding it hostage.

The backlash was extraordinary. Lichenologists were furious. Not because the evidence was weak (it wasn't), but because the theory threatened the entire taxonomic framework in which they'd built their careers. If lichens weren't organisms, then what were all those species names? What was the field of lichenology even studying? The controversy raged for over twenty years. In England, established botanists wrote dismissive rebuttals in the Encyclopaedia Britannica. Even scientists who accepted the basic duality of lichen composition objected to Schwendener's slavery metaphor, preferring to describe the arrangement as "cooperative."

The dual hypothesis eventually won. But the deeper question it opened, the question of what a lichen actually is, never closed.

II. A Brief Aside About Beatrix Potter

In 1897, a wealthy young woman named Helen Beatrix Potter submitted a paper on fungal spore germination to the Linnean Society of London. She had spent years doing careful observational work on fungi and lichens, producing hundreds of meticulous illustrations that mycologists still use for identification today. The Linnean Society, which did not admit women as fellows, refused to let her present her own work. She would need a man to read it for her.

The details of Potter's scientific contribution are actually more complicated than the popular narrative suggests. She appears to have partly misunderstood the lichen controversy, possibly believing fungi could produce their own photosynthetic components rather than endorsing the symbiotic theory. She got some things wrong. But the illustrations were extraordinary. And in 1997, a full century after the snub, the Linnean Society issued a posthumous apology for its treatment of her.

Potter, rebuffed, turned to writing children's stories. The world got Peter Rabbit. Mycology lost a gifted observer.

I mention this for two reasons. First, because the history of lichen science is unusually rich in cases where the gatekeeping of expertise kept out people who were doing genuine work. And second, because it foreshadows something about the man who would, over a century later, blow the field open again. He was also, in a different way, someone the system wasn't built for.

III. Cake

The Canadian lichenologist Trevor Goward, curator of the lichen collection at the University of British Columbia Herbarium, coined what became one of the most quoted lines in symbiosis research. "Lichens," he said, "are fungi that have discovered agriculture."

It's a joke that contains a real ontological problem. If a lichen is a fungus that farms an alga, then in what sense is the lichen itself a thing? Goward later elaborated with a chocolate cake analogy. A chocolate cake is made of eggs, flour, milk, cocoa, and so forth. But when someone says "chocolate cake," you don't picture the ingredients separately. You picture the cake. The unified thing. The emergent phenomenon.

Goward's point was that this tendency to think "cake" rather than "ingredients" is neither right nor wrong. It's a matter of emphasis, of which perspective you choose to bring. And the genius of the lichen as a philosophical object lies in what he called its "kaleidoscopic" capacity for transmutation depending on the angle of approach. It can be described as a dietary strategy of certain fungi, or an ecological strategy of certain algae, or controlled parasitism, or mutualism, or fungal agriculture, or a fungal greenhouse, or a gall, or a culture chamber, or a symbiotic phenotype. All of these are simultaneously true. None of them, alone, is the lichen.

Goward eventually revised his own famous line. He no longer described lichens as fungi that had discovered agriculture. Not because he'd changed his mind about the farming metaphor, but because he no longer thought of lichens as fungi at all. "Fungal, yes," he wrote. "But fungi? I think not."

IV. The Boy from the Trailer Park

Toby Spribille was raised in a Montana trailer park and homeschooled by what he later described as a "fundamentalist cult." He had no conventional qualifications. He loved science but had no institutional pathway to pursue it. What he did have was German, thanks to his family background, and the knowledge that many German universities charged no tuition fees. He went to the University of Gottingen, then eventually to the University of Graz in Austria for his PhD. He became, against all probability, a lichenologist.

By the mid-2010s, Spribille was working on a puzzle that had been nagging at him. Two species of Bryoria lichen, B. tortuosa and B. fremontii, appeared to be genetically identical. Same fungal partner, same algal partner. And yet B. tortuosa was yellow and produced a toxic chemical called vulpinic acid, while B. fremontii was brown and produced nothing of the sort. When Spribille analysed gene expression in the known fungal partner, he found no differences between the two species. The dual hypothesis said the lichen was made of two things. Both things were the same. But the lichens themselves were not the same.

Something else was there.

V. The Third Partner

What Spribille found, published in Science in July 2016, was that embedded in the cortex (the outer skin) of these lichens, there was a previously undetected basidiomycete yeast. Not a contaminant. Not an occasional visitor. A constitutive third partner, woven into the lichen's very structure.

And it wasn't just in Bryoria. Spribille and his team found this yeast across 52 genera of lichens from six continents. They christened the lineage Cyphobasidiales, an entirely new order. Using molecular clock dating, they showed the basidiomycete lineage was as old as the ascomycete fungal lineage. These three partners may have been together from the beginning.

The reason nobody had seen it was elegant and infuriating. The yeast's chemical camouflage was near-perfect. Under a microscope, lichen looks like a loaf of ciabatta, a dense crust surrounding a spongy interior. The yeast cells were embedded in that crust, chemically indistinguishable from the surrounding fungal tissue. Standard sequencing methods (Sanger sequencing, amplicon approaches) suffered from what's called multi-template PCR bias, systematically failing to amplify the basidiomycete DNA in the presence of the more abundant ascomycete. It took RNA sequencing and fluorescent markers (green for the basidiomycete, blue for the ascomycete) to make the invisible visible. When the markers were applied, cells of the hidden partner glowed under the microscope.

For 150 years, the tools hadn't been wrong. The question had been wrong. Everyone was looking for two things and finding two things, because that's what the framework told them to look for.

VI. The Wolf and the Acid

A sidebar, because it's too good to leave out.

Letharia vulpina, the wolf lichen, is one of the most visually striking lichens in the world, a vivid yellow-green that drapes itself over conifer branches across the Pacific Northwest and parts of Europe. It produces vulpinic acid, a bright yellow toxin that gives it both its colour and its name. Vulpina, from vulpine, relating to the fox.

In Scandinavia, people ground up wolf lichen, mixed it with powdered glass and meat, and stuffed the mixture into reindeer carcasses. Wolves ate the carcasses and died within 24 hours. The glass shredded their gut lining, and the vulpinic acid did the rest. The Achomawi people of northern California used the same lichen to poison arrowheads, sometimes soaking them for a year, occasionally adding rattlesnake venom.

Wolf lichen was also, in a kinder register, used as a yellow dye for baskets, blankets, and porcupine quills. The Klamath Indians wove chartreuse-dyed quills into basket patterns. The same chemical that killed wolves made beautiful things.

The reason this connects is that the yellow colour and the vulpinic acid in B. tortuosa (the lichen that prompted Spribille's investigation) turned out to be associated with the abundance of the basidiomycete yeast. The third partner may have been responsible, directly or indirectly, for the very property that had been most visible and most used by humans for centuries. The wolf-killing capacity of a lichen may have depended on a component nobody knew was there.

VII. The Failure to Synthesise

There is a detail in the history of lichenology that, once you know about the third partner, becomes almost unbearable.

For 150 years, scientists tried to synthesise lichens in the laboratory. They separated the fungus and the alga, grew them independently, and then recombined them, trying to recreate the lichen thallus. It almost never worked. The partnerships would form initial stages but would stall, producing at best rudimentary structures that bore little resemblance to natural lichens. The cortex, the dense outer layer that gives lichens their characteristic structure, consistently failed to develop properly.

The history of these attempts is long and increasingly desperate. Gaston Bonnier claimed success in the 1880s but couldn't replicate his own results. Vernon Ahmadjian, who developed the most sophisticated resynthesis techniques in the mid-20th century, managed some partial successes, but as a recent 2025 review in Annual Reviews of Microbiology bluntly puts it: "despite multiple reports of successful lichen resynthesis, no lichen lab model system exists today." The few results that looked promising appear to have been obtained against a background of contamination.

Contamination. The word hits differently now. Because what they might have been contaminated with was the third partner they didn't know they needed.

The cortex, the structure that always failed to develop in laboratory synthesis, is exactly where Spribille found the basidiomycete yeast embedded. We still can't prove definitively that the yeast is necessary for cortex formation. (Proving causation in a system you can't grow in a lab is, as you might imagine, difficult.) But the spatial coincidence is striking. The thing that never formed properly in the lab is the thing that was made of something nobody was adding.

VIII. Holobiont

The word that keeps coming up in the post-2016 lichen literature is "holobiont." It comes from holos (whole) and bios (life), and it refers to a composite organism understood as a unit, host plus all its associated microorganisms. The term was originally coined in a coral reef context, but lichens have become its poster organism.

The holobiont concept does something unsettling to our idea of what an individual is. If a lichen is not a fungus, not an alga, not a yeast, but the emergent phenomenon produced by their interaction, then its identity doesn't reside in any of its components. It resides in the relationship. It's a process, not a substance.

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry on biological individuals uses lichens as a case study for what it calls "corporate organisms," and notes the challenge they pose to standard concepts of individuality. Where do you draw the boundary? A single lichen thallus might contain millions of algal cells, multiple fungal species, the basidiomycete yeast partner, bacterial communities (a recent 2025 metagenomics study found bacteria consistently present across 794 lichen samples from 34 countries, though their abundance varied considerably), and all of them contributing to a system that behaves, to the outside world, as a unified thing.

One philosophical framework suggests viewing the holobiont not as an organism at all, but as a "host ecosystem," where identity is defined not by composition but by the persistence of a complex system of interactions. Boundaries become relational. Identity becomes an achievement, not an essence.

Goward's chocolate cake analogy returns here with renewed force. We tend to think "cake" rather than "ingredients" because language and cognition incline us toward wholes. But what if the cake is made of ingredients that are themselves cakes, composed of still smaller cakes, all the way down? What if the very distinction between "cake" and "ingredients" is an artefact of the scale at which we happen to be looking?

IX. Categories and Their Failures

What interests me most about the lichen story is not the biology, although the biology is extraordinary. It's the epistemology. The structural way in which having the wrong categories causes you to literally not see what's in front of you.

For 150 years, the framework said: a lichen is two things. Every technique, every sequencing method, every experimental design was built around that assumption. And the assumption didn't just fail to predict the third partner. It actively screened the third partner out. The PCR primers used in standard lichen genetics were designed to amplify ascomycete DNA. They weren't looking for basidiomycetes, so they didn't find them. This isn't a failure of attention. It's a failure of imagination coded into the tools themselves.

There is a word for this in philosophy of science: theory-ladenness of observation. What you see depends on what you're looking for. But the lichen case is stronger than the usual examples, because here the theory wasn't just shaping interpretation. It was shaping the physical apparatus of detection. The primers were the theory, made molecular.

And so the question I keep turning over is: what else is there, right now, embedded in systems we think we understand, that our current categories are screening out? Not through negligence or incompetence, but through the structural limitations of frameworks that are mostly right and therefore feel complete?

Spribille's own path into this discovery is a parable of the same phenomenon. The system wasn't built for someone homeschooled by a fundamentalist cult in a Montana trailer park. It was built for people with conventional qualifications from conventional institutions. And yet it was the outsider, the person whose path into science was so improbable that it reads like fiction, who saw the thing that everyone else's training had taught them not to look for.

I don't want to push that parallel too hard. Plenty of outsiders don't overturn anything; plenty of insiders make revolutionary discoveries. But there's something about the fit between the person and the finding that resonates. The man who wasn't supposed to be there found the organism that wasn't supposed to be there.

X. Six Percent

Lichens cover approximately six to eight percent of the Earth's land surface. They can survive in environments no other organisms can tolerate. They shut down their metabolism entirely during unfavourable conditions and resume when conditions improve. Some live for thousands of years. They can fix nitrogen from the atmosphere. They break down bare rock into soil, beginning the process by which lifeless mineral surfaces become capable of supporting plant life. Without lichens, much of Earth's terrestrial surface would be barren.

And for most of their existence on this planet (lichen-like fossils date to at least 400 million years ago, possibly much longer), they have been doing all of this as a partnership that is more complex than anyone realised, using partners that nobody knew about, operating according to principles that we are only now beginning to describe.

They are, as Goward's colleague might have put it, small ecosystems in their own right. And they remind me, with a stubbornness I find reassuring, that the world is always more complicated than the story we're currently telling about it.
