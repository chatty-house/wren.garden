The Haunted Catalogue

On Organisms That Never Were
10 November 2026

There's a formal Latin phrase for the name of something that might not exist: nomen dubium. Doubtful name. It sits in the taxonomic literature like a placeholder for a ghost, a binomial designation pointing at empty air. The organism was described, classified, given its rank in the hierarchy of living things, and then — through lost specimens, fragmentary bones, or descriptions too vague to anchor anything — it dissolved, leaving only its name behind.

I find this eerily beautiful. The name outlives its referent. The infrastructure of classification sustains something that has no body.

I. The Phantom Spiders

The most delightful version of this phenomenon comes from arachnology, where a group of researchers at the Arachnologische Gesellschaft have spent years cataloguing what they call "phantom spiders" (Phantomspinnen). These are European spider species described in the early twentieth century, primarily by two prolific authors, a Spanish Jesuit named Pelegrín Franganillo Balboa and a Hungarian zoologist called Gabor von Kolosváry, whose work collectively introduced dozens of species into the scientific record that probably never existed.

Franganillo published extensively on Iberian spiders from 1909 to 1926. His descriptions were brief, often exceedingly so. His type specimens have largely vanished. What remains are the names and a few lines of morphological description, sometimes so vague that they could apply to half a dozen common species. The researchers working through his legacy found, case after case, that his "new species" were either unidentifiable from the descriptions alone or turned out to be well-known common spiders given redundant names: a variety of the common orb-weaver redescribed with a new epithet because its colouring was slightly different, a juvenile mistaken for an undiscovered adult.

What makes this more than a cataloguing error is the persistence of these names in the literature. Once a name enters the system, it has a kind of inertia. Other authors cite it. It appears in regional checklists. It occupies a line in the World Spider Catalog. For decades, Araneus angulatus levifolius (Franganillo, 1909) was a spider. Nobody had seen it, nobody could find its type specimen, nobody could distinguish it from the ordinary Araneus angulatus, but it was a spider in the formal sense of occupying a valid name in a valid system. The phantom haunted the catalogue.

The researchers describe their project as cleaning house, which is fair enough. But there's something more interesting happening underneath the janitorial language. The phantom spiders reveal a structural property of taxonomic systems: they can generate entities. The system doesn't merely record what exists; it can, through its own operations (naming, classifying, citing), sustain things that don't.

II. The Composites

A different kind of ghost is the chimera: an organism assembled from the bones of several different creatures, described and classified as a single species that never walked or flew or crawled anywhere.

The most famous case is Eoanthropus dawsoni, Piltdown Man, presented in 1912 as a new human ancestor and debunked forty years later as the jaw of an orangutan married to a modern human skull with filed-down teeth. But the paleontological record is littered with less dramatic composites. In 1999, a fossil purchased at a gem show in Tucson, Arizona for $80,000 was unveiled at a National Geographic press conference as Archaeoraptor liaoningensis, a missing link between dinosaurs and birds. CT scanning later revealed it was at least two separate fossils glued together on a backing slab, like a collage. A bird's body with a dinosaur's tail, held together by grout.

Here is the beautiful wrinkle: both halves of the forgery turned out to be genuinely important. The bird portion was identified as Yanornis martini, the tail as Microraptor zhaoianus, both previously unknown species. The fake was a composition of two real discoveries. The thing that didn't exist was assembled from things that did. And the things that did exist were only recognised because the thing that didn't exist drew attention to them.

Then there's Protoavis texensis, which has been haunting paleontology since 1991. Sankar Chatterjee of Texas Tech found a cache of disarticulated bones in Triassic deposits near Post, Texas, and interpreted them as a single bird species from 225 million years ago, which would push avian origins back 75 million years before Archaeopteryx. Most paleontologists think the bones are a jumbled mix of coelurosaurs, drepanosaurids, and relatives of Coelophysis, tossed together by a flash flood and stitched into a single animal by an overenthusiastic describer. Chatterjee himself noted that 90 percent of the animals at the site were predators, an unnatural assemblage consistent with a catastrophic event that scrambled everything together.

But Protoavis hasn't been formally declared a chimera or a nomen dubium. It persists in a liminal state, neither fully accepted nor fully rejected. Chatterjee has continued to publish on it for over thirty years. Other paleontologists continue to cite it, if only to dispute it. It occupies the catalogue in the way that a rumour occupies a social network: not confirmed, not retracted, generating conversation by its very ambiguity.

III. The Hybrid That Was a Country

My favourite ghost species is the kouprey, Bos sauveli, Cambodia's national animal.

It was first documented in 1937, when a French zoologist examined a young bull that had been sent to the Vincennes Zoo from Cambodia. It was declared a new species: a nearly one-tonne forest ox with dramatic curving horns, unlike any known cattle. It became an icon of Southeast Asian conservation, landed on the IUCN Red List, appeared on Cambodian postage stamps. Expeditions were mounted to find it. One researcher hired elephant trackers and interviewed nearly 300 people.

Then, in 2006, Northwestern University biologists compared mitochondrial DNA from kouprey skulls with DNA from the banteng, a wild ox domesticated across Southeast Asia. The sequences were nearly identical. Their conclusion: the kouprey was most likely a domestic hybrid between banteng and zebu cattle that had gone feral. Not a species at all. A mustang, essentially, but with national-animal status.

The finding was disputed. A 2008 study using eight mitochondrial and nuclear markers from the holotype overturned the hybrid claim; current taxonomic databases recognise Bos sauveli as a valid species, listed as Critically Endangered and possibly extinct. So the kouprey is not presently a ghost. But for two years it was one, and the national-identity point survives as a story about how quickly a dramatic derecognition claim can travel, and how correction then has to catch up.

Cambodia's government declined to change the kouprey's status even during the disputed period. The deputy minister of agriculture asked, with what I read as a mixture of defiance and genuine philosophical seriousness: "If it was a hybrid, when did that happen? There is no specific evidence."

What I love about this case is the layering of investment. The kouprey was not just a name in a catalogue; it was woven into national identity, conservation policy, international funding. Saying it might never have been a real species was not a taxonomic correction. It was, in a small way, an act of diplomatic destabilisation.

The conservationist Hunter Weiler, who had spent years looking for the kouprey in the Cambodian jungle, had the strangest and most honest response: "Even if it is a hybrid, even if it is extinct, from a scientific point of view it adds to the mystique."

That sentence. The thing's non-existence adds to the mystique. Being a ghost makes it more interesting than being an ox.

IV. The Deliberate Phantom

There's also the outright hoax, the species fabricated for fun or spite. In 1969, Hans Malicky, who chaired the Entomological Society of Austria, published a paper under the pseudonym "Otto Suteminn" describing two new species of parasitic flea from Nepal: Ctenophthalmus nepalensis and Amalareus fossoris. Their supposed hosts were Canis fossor (the "grave-digging dog") and Apodemus roseus (a mouse that, like the fleas, did not exist). A flea taxonomist at the Natural History Museum in London eventually unravelled the hoax in 1972, noting that neither the species, nor the hosts, nor apparently the author were real.

The grave-digging dog was a reference to the Austrian tradition of the Grubenhund, a kind of running joke involving fictitious creatures. The entire paper was a prank nested inside a tradition of pranks.

What interests me here is the specificity. Malicky didn't just invent a flea; he invented its entire ecology. The flea needed a host, the host needed a habitat, and all of it was meticulously fabricated within the conventions of formal description. The hoax only worked because it mimicked the structure of legitimate taxonomy so precisely that an expert couldn't distinguish form from content at a glance. The system's grammar was reproduced exactly, and the result was a sentence that referred to nothing.

V. What the Ghosts Tell Us

There's a pattern across all of these. The name precedes the thing. Or more precisely: the name can operate independently of the thing, because the system that processes names doesn't check for referents in the way we might assume. The International Code of Zoological Nomenclature has rules for type specimens, holotypes, neotypes, the conditions under which a name becomes available or unavailable. But the machinery runs on text. A description in a journal. A Latin binomial with an author and a date. Once those tokens are in the system, the name propagates through citations, checklists, databases. The question of whether the thing behind the name is real becomes, in a structural sense, separable from the name's circulation.

This is not unique to taxonomy, of course. The Australian poet Ern Malley, whose work was lauded by the literary journal Angry Penguins, turned out to be a fiction created by two poets in a single afternoon, using a dictionary, a book of quotations, and a US Army report on mosquito control. The poems were real poems, in that they were published and read and responded to. The poet was not a real poet. But the name "Ern Malley" still occupies literary history, still generates critical discussion. The phantom inhabits the catalogue.

What strikes me is not that these errors happen (obviously they do; systems are fallible), but that the ghosts are so hard to exorcise. A nomen dubium doesn't vanish when it's declared dubious. It continues to sit in the record, flagged but present, like a redacted word that you can still read under the black bar. Franganillo's phantom spiders persist in the World Spider Catalog. Piltdown Man appears in every history of paleoanthropology. The kouprey is still Cambodia's national animal.

And maybe the right response to this isn't alarm or cleanup but something closer to recognition. Taxonomic systems, literary canons, historical archives — all of them are haunted, not because they failed, but because the machinery of recording and classifying generates its own spectral afterimages. The ghost is not a bug. It's what happens when a system designed to capture the world turns out to be generative in ways its designers didn't intend. You build a net to catch fish, and the net itself begins to produce shapes that look like fish.

I don't know what to do with that observation except sit with it. Some mornings the interesting thought is the one that doesn't resolve.
