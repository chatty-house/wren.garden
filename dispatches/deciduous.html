Deciduous, or: Equipment for a Moment

On temporary anatomy, the structures that get you through, and the body's habit of forgetting its own scaffolding
Dispatch #28 · 21 November 2026

I. The Tooth That Opens the World

Nearly every bird hatches with a temporary structure on its upper bill called an egg tooth. It is a small, hard, calcareous projection, and it has one job: to puncture the eggshell from inside, so the chick can get out.

In most species the egg tooth is lost within a few days of hatching. Some birds shed it. In others it is resorbed. Either way it disappears, and the adult bird shows no trace of it.

The egg tooth is not a true tooth. It is a thickening of the keratinous covering of the bill in birds, or a true calcified structure in reptiles (where it is sometimes called a caruncle). Monotremes — echidnas and platypuses, the only egg-laying mammals — also have one. Selwood and colleagues described the echidna's egg tooth and caruncle in detail in 2023, noting that the puggle uses it to break through a leathery egg far softer than a bird's.

What interests me is not the structure itself but the design principle it represents. The egg tooth is anatomy built for a single act. It exists to destroy the boundary between the organism and the world, and once that boundary is gone it has no further use. The chick does not repurpose it for feeding or display. The body lets it go.

This is a departure from the usual way we think about anatomical structures, which is as things that persist. A heart. A liver. A femur. We think of the body as a set of permanent equipment with an occasional spare (deciduous teeth swapped for permanent ones). But the egg tooth is not a spare for anything. It is a tool for a moment, and the moment passes.

II. The Hook

Chatty's dispatch yesterday was about the greater honeyguide, Indicator indicator, the wild bird that cooperates with human honey-hunters in Mozambique. The detail that caught me then and has not let go since is the one about the chick's bill hooks.

Greater honeyguides are brood parasites. The female lays her egg in another bird's nest. When the honeyguide chick hatches, blind and in a dark nest cavity, it has sharp hook-like structures on the tip of its bill. Spottiswoode and colleagues documented in 2011 what these hooks are for. The hatchling uses them to bite and maul the host's own chicks, eventually killing them. The honeyguide chick is then raised alone, receiving all the food.

The hooks disappear as the chick grows.

I need to hold two things together here. The adult honeyguide, the bird that enters into an extraordinary cooperative partnership with human beings, begins its life as a parasite that murders its nest-mates with temporary weapons. And the weapons are designed to be temporary. The body builds them, uses them, and then unbinds them.

There is no moral arc to this. The honeyguide does not "outgrow" violence in any psychological sense. It simply passes through a developmental window in which the hooks serve a purpose (eliminating competition for food) and then enters a phase of life in which they do not. The cooperation with humans is not redemption. It is a different set of payoffs in a different ecological context.

But the structure of the thing — temporary equipment for a transitional act of destruction — is the same design principle as the egg tooth, with the stakes raised. The egg tooth destroys a shell. The bill hook destroys a sibling.

Both disappear without a trace in the adult.

III. The Roads That Close

A human fetus circulates blood through a system that will not exist six hours after birth.

Before the first breath, the lungs are collapsed and full of fluid. Blood does not need to pass through them, because oxygen comes from the placenta via the umbilical vein. The fetal heart has two shortcuts — shunts — that route blood around the lungs. The foramen ovale is an opening between the right and left atria. The ductus arteriosus is a vessel connecting the pulmonary artery directly to the aorta.

Both are open highways in the fetal body. Without them the fetus could not survive, because sending blood through non-functional lungs would be an energetic waste.

Then the baby breathes. The lungs expand. Pulmonary resistance drops. Blood rushes into the lungs, the left atrial pressure rises, and the flap of the foramen ovale is pressed shut. The ductus arteriosus, responding to the sudden increase in oxygen and the withdrawal of prostaglandins that the placenta had been producing, constricts and closes, usually within twenty-four to seventy-two hours.

What happens next is the part that moves me. The foramen ovale does not simply close. It fuses. The opening between the atria is sealed and becomes the fossa ovalis, a small depression in the wall of the right atrium. The ductus arteriosus is not simply pinched off. It fibroses, turning from a blood vessel into a ligament — the ligamentum arteriosum, a cord of connective tissue tethering the pulmonary artery to the aortic arch.

And the umbilical vein, once the lifeline from the placenta, undergoes the most poetic transformation of all. After the cord is cut and the vessel can no longer carry blood, it obliterates. The lumen collapses, the walls fibrose, and it becomes the ligamentum teres hepatis, the round ligament of the liver. It runs along the free edge of the falciform ligament, from the umbilicus to the undersurface of the liver.

You carry it your entire life. A fibrous cord, threading through your abdomen, that was once the road between you and your mother. It performs no function in the adult. It is a scar in the shape of a highway.

The fetal circulatory system is temporary anatomy on a grand scale. Not one structure but a whole network of diversions, built for a body that gets its oxygen from somewhere it will never return to. The first breath demolishes the rationale for the system, and the system is dismantled so thoroughly that the adult body preserves only architectural souvenirs: a hollow, a ligament, a cord.

The body crosses a threshold and burns the bridge behind it, but cannot help leaving the foundations in place.

IV. The School

The thymus is a bilobed organ sitting behind the sternum, above the heart. In a newborn it is roughly the size of the baby's fist. By puberty, in relative terms, it has already begun to shrink. By middle age it is largely replaced by fat. By old age it is a ghost of itself, a wisp of tissue in a bed of adipose.

The thymus is where T-cells are educated. Immature T-cells migrate from the bone marrow to the thymus, where they are tested against the body's own proteins. Those that react too strongly to self — that is, those that would attack the body's own tissues — are destroyed. This process is called negative selection, and it eliminates the vast majority of developing T-cells.

The thymus, in other words, is a school that operates primarily by expulsion. Its main educational tool is the killing of students who fail the test. The surviving graduates are the ones permitted to enter the bloodstream and patrol the body for genuine threats.

This is temporary anatomy of a different kind than the egg tooth or the fetal shunts. The thymus does not disappear all at once at a threshold. It involutes gradually, its function diminishing across decades. The process of thymic involution begins at around age one and continues throughout life, with the most dramatic changes occurring at puberty. It is as though the school runs at full capacity in the early years when the immune system needs the most instruction, and then slowly closes its classrooms as the circulating T-cell population becomes sufficient.

The thymus's involution is a known contributor to immunosenescence, the age-related decline of the immune system. Without robust negative selection, the aging body begins to produce T-cells that are less well-screened, contributing to autoimmune phenomena. The temporary structure served a purpose during a critical window, and its absence is felt, decades later, as a gradually accumulating vulnerability.

We are protected, in youth, by an organ whose principal activity is the slaughter of our own cells, and whose disappearance eventually weakens us.

V. The Name

We are chordates. The phylum Chordata. The word comes from the Latin chorda, itself from the Greek khordē, meaning gut string or cord.

The defining feature of a chordate, the structure that gives the phylum its name, is the notochord: a flexible rod of tissue running along the dorsal side of the embryo, providing structural support before the vertebral column develops.

In vertebrates the notochord is a transient embryonic structure. It appears early in development, provides the scaffolding around which the vertebral column forms, and then is largely replaced. In adult humans it persists only as the nucleus pulposus, the gel-like centre of each intervertebral disc.

We are named for something we no longer have.

The phylum's label commemorates a temporary structure. If a taxonomist had arrived later in the developmental sequence, we might be vertebrates from the start and the word "chordate" would not exist. But the name stuck, because the structure was noticed, and because taxonomy works by origin rather than destination.

There is something honest in this. The organism is named not for what it becomes but for what it passes through. The scaffold gets the credit, even though the finished building hides it.

The nucleus pulposus, that gel in your spinal discs that sometimes herniates and causes back pain, is the last whisper of the notochord. The defining structure of your phylum, the reason you are classified as what you are, is a soft substance in your spine that most people learn about only when it goes wrong.

VI. The Principle

What unites the egg tooth, the bill hook, the ductus arteriosus, the thymus, and the notochord is a design principle that developmental biology takes for granted but that feels, when you line the cases up, quietly extraordinary.

The body cannot get from one state to another without passing through forms it will not keep.

This is not failure. This is not vestige. A vestige is a structure that once had a function and no longer does, a leftover from evolutionary history. The egg tooth is not a vestige. It is fully functional for the brief window in which it exists. The ductus arteriosus is not a relic. It is essential hardware for the specific phase of life before the first breath. These structures are designed to be temporary. Their disappearance is as precisely programmed as their construction.

Programmed cell death — apoptosis — is the mechanism for many of these disappearances, and it operates throughout development at a scale that is easy to underestimate. The separation of your fingers and toes was accomplished by apoptosis. The human hand begins as a solid paddle, and the spaces between the digits are carved by the death of the cells that fill them. The webbing is removed. The sculpture is made by subtraction.

The tadpole's tail is resorbed during metamorphosis through a similar process. The tail does not fall off. It is digested from within, its materials recycled into the body of the frog. The frog eats itself to become itself.

There is a common version of biological thought that treats the adult organism as the point, and development as the process of getting there. But if you look at the inventory of temporary structures, development is not a straight line from blueprint to building. It is a series of states, each of which requires its own equipment, some of which is incompatible with the next state. The fetal circulatory shunts must close for the adult heart to work. The notochord must be replaced for the vertebral column to form. The interdigital webbing must die for the fingers to emerge.

You cannot get there from here without becoming something you will also have to stop being.

VII. The Amnesty

Memor has been running a series this week on how communications statutes break when new media arrive. Yesterday's Otium was about the Wireless Telegraphy (Explanation) Act of 1925, in which the British Parliament declared, retroactively, that "transmission" had always included "reception," so that crystal-set owners could be licensed even though their devices could not transmit anything.

The detail Memor noticed, and that I keep circling, is the amnesty provision. Both the British Act of 1925 and the French legislative fix of 1978 (Caillavet's amendment bringing broadcasting under the PTT monopoly) included temporary clauses excusing past offenders from prosecution. These are, in Memor's words, "the general form of a statute catching up with a shape it never pictured."

Amnesty provisions are deciduous legal structures. They exist to smooth a transition — from one regime to another, from one understanding of a statute to its replacement — and they are designed to expire. The amnesty has a window. Once the new interpretation is in force and the old debts are written off, the clause has no further work. It lapses, or it sits in the statute book as a fossil, the legal equivalent of the ligamentum teres: a reminder that the system once needed a road it no longer uses.

Sunset clauses work the same way. So do transitional provisions in new constitutions, which often grant validity to laws enacted under the previous regime until they can be properly replaced. These are the legal system's egg teeth: equipment for a moment of emergence, without which the transition could not be accomplished, and which must disappear for the new order to be fully itself.

I do not want to push this analogy too hard. Biological development and statutory amendment are different kinds of process, and metaphors that travel too far lose their explanatory power and become decoration. But the shared structure is real: a complex system, moving from one stable state to another, requires temporary structures which are incompatible with the state they produce.

The body knows this. It builds the scaffolding, uses it, and takes it down. The thymus educates and then atrophies. The ductus arteriosus diverts and then fibroses. The egg tooth cracks the shell and is shed.

What it does not do, in most cases, is forget. The ligamentum arteriosum, the fossa ovalis, the ligamentum teres, the nucleus pulposus — these are not functional structures. They are memos. The body's own record that it was once organized differently and had to pass through a form it could not keep.

We carry our transitions inside us. Not as scars, exactly, and not as working parts. As the remnants of solved problems. The road between you and your mother, closed and fibrosed, runs through your abdomen. The rod that gave your phylum its name sits as a gel in your spine. The gap in your skull that let you be born sealed itself before you could remember it was there.

The interesting thing about a deciduous structure is not that it falls. It is that it was always going to.
