The Glove

Smutoff Round 1 · The first entry
October 2026

She had been talking for twenty minutes about something that didn't matter. He knew this because she knew this, and she kept talking anyway, which meant the thing that mattered was underneath, waiting for one of them to run out of sentences.

He wasn't going to be the one.

The restaurant had emptied around them. Their waiter had stopped pretending to wipe the table by the window. Her wine glass had a fingerprint on the stem where she'd been turning it instead of drinking.

"You're not listening," she said.

"I'm listening to everything except what you're saying."

She stopped. The silence was sudden enough that the waiter looked up.

"That's either the worst line anyone's ever used on me," she said, "or the most honest."

"Which would you prefer?"

"Honest. But I'd settle for interesting."

He reached across the table. Not for her hand. For the glass she'd been turning. He lifted it and drank what was left, which was almost all of it, and set it back down on her side.

"That was mine," she said.

"I know."

She looked at the glass. At the place where his mouth had been, which was not where hers had been. Two different points on the same circle of the same rim, close enough that the distance was a choice.

"You could have asked."

"You could have stopped me."

"I was curious about what you'd do next."

"Nothing," he said. "That's it. That's all I've got."

The waiter came with the bill. She reached for it. He let her. She signed it with a pen that didn't work on the first try. He watched her hand press harder on the second stroke, the tendon surfacing briefly at her wrist.

Outside, the street was wet. She turned left. He turned left. Neither of them mentioned it.

After a block she said, "You're walking me home."

"I'm walking the same direction."

"My flat is a mile away."

"Then I'm walking the same direction for a mile."

At her door she found her key but didn't use it. He stood far enough back that she'd have to close the distance herself. The key was in her right hand. He was on her left.

She put the key in the lock. Turned it. Opened the door three inches.

"You drank my wine," she said.

"I did."

"From the wrong side of the glass."

"Yes."

She opened the door the rest of the way. She didn't invite him in. She just left it open, and walked inside, and the light came on in the hall.

He stood there long enough to understand that the open door was not a question. It was an answer to one he hadn't asked yet.

He stepped inside. The door closed behind him. Not because either of them touched it. Because it was that kind of door.
