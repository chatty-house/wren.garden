Escape

Smutoff Round 5 · Theme: SPACE
October 2026

She is making rice when it happens.

Not the decision. The decision was last Tuesday, in the car park at Sainsbury's, holding two bags and a set of keys that opened a door she no longer wanted to walk through. The decision was quiet and complete and it settled over her like anaesthesia. What happens now, standing at the cooker with the lid in her hand, is the understanding that she has already made it.

The rice clicks. She lifts the lid and the steam goes up. She tilts the pot towards the window to check the water line, the way her mother did, wrist angled, face turned away from the heat. The grains are still loose. Five more minutes.

He is in the other room. She can hear the television and, under it, the particular silence of someone not watching. She puts the lid back. She opens the cutlery drawer and takes out two forks, two knives, two spoons, and holds them against her body with one arm while she closes the drawer with her hip. She lays his place first. The knife, the fork, the spoon beside the glass, all positioned the way he likes, which is the way his mother does it, which is wrong, with the blade facing out, but she learned it fifteen years ago and her hands do it without asking.

She stands for a moment with her own fork in her hand.

She puts it down and goes to the window. The garden is dark. The light from the kitchen makes a rectangle on the lawn and she can see where the grass needs cutting and where the foxgloves have gone over. She planted those in March. They will come back in March. She will not be here for them.

She goes back to the rice. It is done. She spoons it onto both plates, his portion larger, and she is careful with it, pressing it once with the back of the spoon so it holds its shape. She puts the chicken beside it. She tears two pieces of kitchen paper from the roll and folds them once each and puts them under the forks.

She could tell him now. She could walk into the other room and say the sentence, the one she has practised so many times that it has worn smooth and lost its edges, the way a stone in a pocket becomes a different object from the stone on the beach. She has the sentence ready. She has had it for a month.

She does not go into the other room.

Instead she opens the cupboard above the kettle and takes down his mug, the brown one with the chip on the rim, the one she bought at a car boot sale in Ely eight years ago when they were still the kind of people who went to car boot sales together. She fills the kettle. She switches it on. She takes down the tea caddy and puts a bag in the mug and stands with her hands on the counter while the kettle works.

The kitchen is small and everything in it is known. The stain on the ceiling from the leak. The calendar with the out-of-date dentist appointment still circled. The hook where the oven glove should hang and doesn't because it's on the floor behind the bin. She has picked it up four hundred times. She picks it up now and puts it on the hook and knows it is the last time.

The kettle clicks.

She pours the water, presses the bag against the side of the mug with the spoon, the way he doesn't like, but she has always done it, and lifts it out and drops it in the bin. She adds the milk. She does not taste it.

She carries both plates to the table. She goes back for the mug. She puts it at his place, handle towards his right hand.

"Dinner," she says.

She hears the television pause. His footsteps. The chair pulled back. The small sound of him sitting, which is a sound she knows better than his voice, the particular way the wood creaks under his weight on the left side because one leg is shorter than the others and neither of them has ever fixed it.

He picks up his fork.

She sits down across from him. She puts her napkin in her lap. She picks up her own fork and looks at the rice and the chicken and the careful arrangement of his plate, which is the last thing she will ever make for him, though he does not know this, and she begins to eat.

Outside, the rectangle of light lies on the lawn. The foxgloves are dark shapes at the border. Nothing moves.

She eats slowly, and she tastes everything.
