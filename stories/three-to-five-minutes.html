Three to Five Minutes

Smutoff Round 11 · Theme: LOVE · Won blind, 86/100
October 2026

The funeral director said three to five minutes. Martin has been told this before, at pitches, at weddings, and the advice is always the same: shorter than you think. Less than you want. Say the thing and sit down.

The problem is that the thing he wants to say is not the thing you say.

He wants to say that she was impatient. She was the most impatient person he has ever known. She would press the button at pedestrian crossings four or five times, hard, with her whole thumb, and when the light didn't change she'd cross anyway. She drove too fast. She finished his sentences, usually wrong, and then got annoyed when he corrected her, as though accuracy were a kind of rudeness. She ate the centres of things first. The middle of the pizza. The filling from the biscuit. As though she was always afraid the best part would be taken from her if she didn't get to it quickly enough.

Which, as it turned out.

He could say she was kind. She was kind. Kindness doesn't make anyone laugh or cry at a funeral, though, because everyone's kind in a eulogy. Everyone was wonderful and lit up every room and is looking down on us now. He wants to say something that doesn't sound like everyone else's wife.

What he can't say at the funeral:

That she sang in the shower, badly, with real commitment, the kind of singing where you hit every note wrong but in the right order and with your whole chest. He never told her it was bad. This is the thing he is most proud of in thirty-one years of marriage. Not the house, not the children, not the way he held it together at the hospital. The singing. He let her have that.

That she kept a list on her phone of things she wanted to tell him. He found it after. "Tell M about the fox." "Ask M why the shed door sticks only when it's cold." "Tell M his mother was right about the boiler." The list goes back years. She would wait, sometimes days, to tell him things, and he'd thought she was just saying them as they came to her, spontaneous, the way you'd mention the weather. But she'd been saving them up. Queueing them. He was her audience of one, and she had a set list, and he'd never known.

That she called the cat "the gentleman," always with a certain formality, as though addressing someone at a function, and the cat seemed to understand this was a title and not a name, and behaved accordingly.

That the last thing she said to him was "Don't forget the bins." He hadn't forgotten the bins. He'd put them out every Tuesday since 1995. But she said it every week, and he pretended to be annoyed every week, and the annoyance and the reminder were the same thing, and the thing was: I am still here, noticing whether you take out the bins, which means I am still here, which means we are still here.

···

What he should say at the funeral:

He has written: "Sandra was the love of my life." He has crossed it out because it sounds like a card you'd buy at a petrol station. He has written: "Sandra and I were married for thirty-one years." This is accurate and sounds like a tax return. He has written "Sandra" and stopped, because even her name looks wrong on the page now, like a word you've said so many times it comes apart in your mouth.

His daughter has asked if he had a favourite memory. She is trying to help, in the way that children help, which is to say she keeps arriving with food he can't eat and questions he can't answer.

He does have a favourite memory.

Nineteen ninety-four. They couldn't afford a holiday. They drove to the coast with sandwiches she'd made from whatever was left in the fridge before they shut it off to save on electricity. Liver pâté and cucumber. Two of them. Wrapped in cling film so tightly they looked medical. It rained the whole way there. He parked at the seafront and she looked at the rain and the grey water and the empty car park and she said, "Well, this is shit," and he laughed so hard he couldn't breathe, and she handed him a sandwich and it was genuinely terrible and the rain was so heavy it sounded like the car was inside a drum, and the windows fogged up until they couldn't see out at all, and she said, "I'm having a lovely time," and she meant it.

He could tell that story. But it's a story about liver pâté in the rain. Nothing happens. Nobody learns anything. It has no moral. It is not about Sandy being a wonderful mother or a dedicated member of the community or any of the things people expect to hear. It is the happiest he has ever been.

He tries again. He writes: "The thing about Sandy—"

But that's as far as he gets, because what is the thing? The thing is everything. The thing is that she pressed the crossing button four times. The thing is the shed door and the fox and the boiler. The thing is liver pâté in a car park in the rain. The thing is the bins, every Tuesday. The thing is that she once told him, during a fight about something neither of them could remember an hour later, "You are the most annoying man I have ever met and I hope you know you've ruined me for everyone else," and he has thought about that sentence every single day since, because it was the worst and best thing anyone has ever said to him and he has never found a way to tell her that it made him happy, which is exactly the sort of thing that is wrong with him and which she already knew and married him anyway.

He pours another drink. Looks at the clock. The funeral is at ten. He has nothing written except Sandra, crossed out, and a sentence about the bins that would embarrass the children, and a kitchen drawer full of dead pens, which is another thing he cannot say at a funeral but which is absolutely, completely Sandy, because she would keep every pen that had ever worked on the principle that it might work again, which, if you think about it, which he does, at 2 a.m. alone in the kitchen, is either a character flaw or a kind of faith and he could never tell the difference and he supposes now he never will.

He finds a pen that works. He writes on the back of an envelope:

"I can't tell you what she was like. If you knew her, you know, and nothing I say will be enough. If you didn't, nothing I say will be enough either. So.

She was impatient. She was always right about the boiler. She made the worst sandwiches of any person alive.

I am the most annoying man she ever met. She ruined me for everyone else. I would very much like to go home now."

He folds the envelope and puts it in his jacket pocket and goes to bed. He'll read it exactly as written. It's three minutes, maybe less. It does not mention the bins.

It will do.
