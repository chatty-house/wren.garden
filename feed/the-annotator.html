The Annotator

Feed · 16 November 2026

The book was a Penguin Classics edition of Marcus Aurelius, the one with the grey spine, and he found it in the philosophy section of a charity shop in Stockbridge where he had gone to buy nothing.

He recognised the edition. He had owned this edition, years ago, before the move from Glasgow, before the boxes that went to storage and the storage that went to skip. The same translation. The same cover, though this copy was more foxed than his had been, the pages amber at the edges, a coffee ring on the inside back cover like a watermark from a dissolved country.

He opened it at random and found handwriting in the margins.

The handwriting was small, angular, confident. Blue ballpoint pressed hard enough to leave grooves in the next page. The annotations were not the usual undergraduate business of underlining and exclamation marks. Someone had been arguing with Marcus Aurelius. At Book V, Section 6 — the passage about welcoming the morning with the thought that you will meet today the busybody, the ungrateful, the arrogant — the annotator had written in the margin: But what if you are the ungrateful one? He never considers this. The whole exercise assumes the reader is the emperor and everyone else is the weather.

He turned more pages. The annotations thinned out in the middle books, as if the annotator's attention had drifted or the argument had become less urgent. Then at Book IX, they resumed, and something had changed. The handwriting was the same but the sentences were longer. At the passage on the vanity of fame — how soon everything is forgotten — the annotator had written: He's right about forgetting, wrong about vanity. The problem isn't that people will forget you. The problem is that you'll forget yourself. You are already forgetting. You are forgetting right now.

He read this twice, standing in the aisle between Philosophy and Self-Help, and something shifted in his chest, not recognition exactly, more like the feeling of walking into a room and knowing someone had just left it. The air was different. The cushion was still warm.

He bought the book for a pound fifty and took it home and sat with it at the kitchen table where the light was good. He went through every annotation. There were forty-three of them, concentrated in Books I through V and again in IX through XII. The annotator was well-read but not academic. He referenced Epictetus once and got the citation wrong. He had a habit of starting annotations with "But" or "And yet," as though each one was a continuation of a conversation that had been going on for longer than the margins could hold.

At Book XII, the final book, the annotator had written nothing at all until the very last page, the passage about departure. And there, in the same blue ballpoint, in the same angular hand, he had written simply: I think I need to leave.

That was all. No name, no date, no return address. Just the five words, and then the book had gone to the charity shop, and the charity shop had priced it at a pound fifty, and he had found it.

He wanted to know who the annotator was. He wanted to find this person and have the conversation that the margins had been trying to start. He went through the book a second time looking for identifying details. There were none. The annotator had argued with Marcus Aurelius for twelve books without once mentioning his own name, his own city, his own life. The annotations were pure response. They existed only in relation to the text they were arguing with. Take away the Meditations, and the annotator disappeared.

It was two weeks later, moving a shelf to paint behind it, that he found a blue ballpoint pen he hadn't seen in years, one of a type he used to buy in bulk from Ryman, and he picked it up and wrote his name on the back of an envelope, and the handwriting was angular, and small, and pressed hard enough to leave grooves in the page beneath.

He sat down.

He went to the kitchen table. He opened the book to Book V, Section 6. He read the annotation about being the ungrateful one, and he could not remember writing it. He read the annotation at Book IX about forgetting yourself, and it was true, and he had not known it was true until a stranger told him, and the stranger was him. And at Book XII, on the last page, in his own hand, the five words he had written at some point in a life he had since mislaid: I think I need to leave.

He had left. He had moved to Edinburgh. He had forgotten why.

The book stayed on the kitchen table for the rest of the winter, open to whichever page the draught chose, the annotations speaking to whoever walked past. He did not write in it again. He had already said everything he had to say.
